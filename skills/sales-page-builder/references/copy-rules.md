# Copy Rules

## Diagnose Before Writing

| Situation | What was given | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Blank** | No draft, or generic copy with no point of view | Build the argument from the brief. You choose the angle, but never invent a fact |
| **Real voice present** | A draft with an opinion, a recognizable voice, or a clear angle, even if it's rough | Keep their argument and vocabulary. Fix the buried lead, vague lines, AI tells, and structure |
| **Point edit** | "Fix this headline," "tighten this section" | Change only what was asked, plus the minimum around it to make it fit |

State the diagnosis in one line before drafting. On an existing page, lines that already work are locked from the start. Before delivering, check each change: if you can't say why it's better, revert it.

## Draft Unit By Unit

Break the page into units: headline, subhead, each section, each CTA, the proof lines, and the form microcopy. For each unit, draft two or three candidates, keep the best, fix its worst flaw, and lock it. Then read the assembled page once for flow and repetition.

## The Three-Question Gate

Run every line through three questions:

1. **Can the reader picture it?** A scene, object, or specific situation beats an abstraction.
2. **Can it be proven true or false?** "Cuts the weekly report from four hours to one" is a claim. "Best-in-class" is decoration.
3. **Could a competitor write this exact sentence?** If yes, it's too generic.

A line that passes all three stays. Two passes, sharpen the weak one. One or none, rewrite it from the brief or mark it `[NEED: ...]`.

Ration the punch. Most lines should be plain and specific. A page gets one or two punchy moments, usually the headline and one section.

## Claims Come From The Brief

- Write only what the brief supports. No invented statistics, customers, results, testimonials, logos, guarantees, bonuses, deadlines, or seat limits.
- **Decline with an unlock.** When the user asks for a claim the brief can't support, don't write it and don't silently drop it. Name the claim, say what's missing, offer the closest supportable version, and name the evidence that would unlock the original. Example: "'Clients save 10 hours a week' needs a measured client result. Closest supportable line: 'Built to take a repeated weekly job off your desk.' Unlock: one client's before-and-after time."
- Conditional beats false certainty. "If this job takes you two hours a week..." is honest. "This job takes you two hours a week" is an assumption.
- Quotation marks only around real quotes from the brief, attributed as the brief allows.

## CTAs And Microcopy

- The button says what actually happens: "Book a 20-minute call" only when the click opens a calendar. "Send my details," "Apply," "Get the template," "Join for $X/month."
- Under the button: what happens next, when, and what happens to their details.
- One primary CTA label per page, repeated identically.

## Voice

- Match the user's voice sample: sentence length, punctuation habits, words they use, how paragraphs open. The sample wins over these defaults.
- Without a sample: plain, direct, second person, short paragraphs, specific nouns.
- Use the buyer language from the brief where it fits.

## AI Tells To Remove

- "It's not X, it's Y" reveals and "X, not Y" contrast lines used as a formula. One deliberate contrast on a page is fine. Several read as a template.
- Staccato fragment triads ("One workflow. Three weeks. Done.") used repeatedly.
- "No X, no Y, no Z" lists.
- Hype words carrying the sentence: transform, revolutionize, unlock, supercharge, game-changing, 10x, ultimate, seamless.
- Vague abstractions: streamline, optimize, solutions, empower, leverage.
- Em dashes, unless the user's voice sample uses them.
- Sentences that trail into extra comma clauses.
- A closing line that restates the section instead of moving the reader to act.
