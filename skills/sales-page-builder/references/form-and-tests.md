# Form Check And Test Ideas

Two deeper passes for when the audit needs them. Use the form check when the page's main action is a form: an inquiry, an application, a booking request, a signup, or a checkout. Use the test ideas when the user has enough traffic to compare two versions of a page.

Neither pass overrides the guardrails. No invented proof near the form, no urgency the offer doesn't have, and no promised lift from any change.

## Form Check

### Start With What Happens Next

Before judging fields, find out:

- What kind of form it is: inquiry or application, booking request, signup or lead magnet, or checkout.
- What happens after someone submits it, and how soon. The form and the copy around it have to match that.
- Which fields the user actually reads or uses when they follow up. A field nobody uses is a field to cut.

### Fields

- **Every field has to earn its place.** For each one, ask whether the user needs it before they can reply, whether they could get it later, and whether they could get it another way. There's no fixed field limit (see item 15 in the audit checklist).
- **Open-ended fields need help.** A "tell me about your workflow" box should show an example of a good answer and say roughly how much to write. Without one, visitors guess, write too little, or stop.
- **Mark optional fields as optional.** If a field is required, the reason should be obvious or stated.
- **Choices with a few options** work better as visible options (radio buttons) than a dropdown. Use a dropdown for long lists, and add "Other" with a text box when the list can't cover everyone.
- **Phone numbers** stay optional unless the next step is a phone call. If required, say why.
- **Email** is one field, with no "confirm your email" field.

### Labels And Layout

- Keep labels visible above each field. A placeholder that disappears when someone starts typing leaves them unsure what the field was. Use placeholders for an example answer.
- One column. Two fields side by side only when they belong together, like first and last name.
- The button sits right after the last field.
- On mobile, fields and the button need to be easy to tap, and each field should open the right keyboard (email, phone, number).
- Split a form into steps only when it's long or the questions fall into clearly separate groups. Show progress (step 2 of 3), allow going back, and don't lose answers on refresh.

### The Button

- The button says what happens and what the visitor gets: "Send my workflow," "Get the guide," "Request a quote." "Submit" says neither.
- The button text matches what the form actually starts. If the button says "Book a call," the next screen offers a time.

### Errors And After Submit

- Error messages sit next to the field, say what's wrong, and say how to fix it ("Add an email like name@company.com"). Never clear what the visitor already typed.
- After submit, confirm it worked and restate the next step and when it happens. If the page promised a call, this is where the booking link goes.

### Near The Form

What a visitor might want to see right before they act, only when it's true and in the brief:

- What happens to their details.
- When they'll hear back.
- One real proof line, if the brief has one. Never an invented one.
- For a signup or download, what they'll receive and how often.

### What To Measure

If the user has analytics, these show where the form loses people: form views, how many start the first field, submissions, and the split by device. Without field-level data, suggest the minimum in the checklist's Not Scored section.

## Test Ideas By Page Type

Suggest these as experiments, never as fixes, and only when the page gets enough traffic to compare two versions. On a low-traffic page, suggest judging a change by the quality of the inquiries or sales conversations instead. Each idea names the question it answers.

### Any Sales Page

- **Headline:** a specific outcome vs a broader promise. Does naming the exact result bring in the right buyers?
- **Button on the first screen:** shown vs held until the offer is explained. Do visitors need context first?
- **Proof placement:** a real result near the top vs in its own section vs next to the form. Where does proof help the decision most?
- **Asks along the page:** one ask at the end vs the same ask repeated after key sections, especially after the FAQ.
- **Page length:** short and direct vs the full argument. Warm visitors often need less than cold ones.
- **Navigation:** kept vs removed, so the page has one path.
- **Section order:** moving the strongest section higher.

### Services And Consulting

- **Price signal:** "starts at" a number vs "fixed price, quoted after the call." Does showing a number filter out bad-fit inquiries?
- **The next step:** a calendar to book a call vs a form that gets a reply. Make sure the button promises whichever one is real.
- **Inquiry questions:** adding one question about the buyer's main problem, to see if replies get easier to qualify.

### Courses, Cohorts, And Workshops

- **Curriculum detail:** a short outcome list vs a full week-by-week plan.
- **A real deadline**, if one exists (enrollment closes, the cohort starts). Test how prominently to show it. Never test an invented one.
- **Guarantee placement**, only if the guarantee is real: near the price vs in the FAQ.

### Digital Products And Templates

- **Showing the product:** screenshots vs a short walkthrough video vs a sample file.
- **Buy button vs a details page first.**

### Memberships And Paid Newsletters

- **What the reader gets:** a list of what arrives and how often vs examples of past issues.
- **Monthly vs yearly price shown first.**

### Lead Magnets And Signups

- **Email only vs email and name.**
- **What they get:** a preview of the inside of the guide vs a description of it.

### Matching Where Visitors Come From

- **One page per traffic source:** a version whose headline matches the post, email, or ad that sent the visitor there.
- **Audience-specific pages:** different pages for clearly different buyers, when the brief names more than one.
