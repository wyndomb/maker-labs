# Source and voice

## The input is the voice sample

For a newsletter, infer voice from the complete article: vocabulary, formality, energy, sentence and paragraph rhythm, first/second person, questions, fragments, repetition, humor, metaphors, transitions, teaching style, personal disclosure, and certainty.

For rough ideas or a voice note, keep the person's natural language, expressed opinions, emphasis, and spoken energy. Remove nonsemantic filler and transcription noise where meaning is clear; preserve hedges such as “maybe,” “I think,” and “I might try” when they express uncertainty or tentative intent. Organize the thought for the destination without making the speaker more polished, provocative, certain, corporate, or motivational.

A terse bullet list does not establish a rich persona. Use its vocabulary and level of certainty, then write clear, restrained prose with the channel structure. Do not manufacture slang, humor, vulnerability, stories, or quirks to compensate for a small sample. Do not require a voice guide or search for someone's past writing unless requested.

If a newsletter quotes another person, separate the author's narrative voice from the quoted speaker. Never turn a guest's experience or a cited case study into the author's first-person story. If source authorship is ambiguous and materially affects attribution, clarify it within the short interview.

Voice matching is a filter on each draft, not a list of mannerisms to insert. Use brief phrases from the source when helpful; do not copy long sentences merely to sound faithful. Channel structures guide order and pacing, not personality.

## Evidence boundary

Maintain source references for the facts used in each draft, outside the copy. The working brief should distinguish:

- Explicit experiences, opinions, examples, and results.
- Advice or implications directly supported by the source.
- Proposals and hypotheses that have not been tested.
- Unknowns and unclear transcript fragments.

Never invent personal experiences, reader reactions, quotations, statistics, results, dates, capabilities, opinions, emotional responses, or certainty. Preserve material timeframes, conditions, sample sizes, and limitations.

Editorial development can clarify reasoning already present. It cannot turn a vague idea into a fabricated case study. If an example is hypothetical in the source, keep it hypothetical. If a prompt is copied, preserve it accurately; label an adapted instruction as an adaptation, not a verbatim quote.

Current product behavior, prices, availability, and similar unstable facts need verification before being presented as current. The main agent may verify the specific claim with an authoritative source when browsing is available. Record verification separately. External research may establish facts, never the author's experience or opinion. If verification is unavailable or contradicts the input, flag it, retain clearly dated historical context when accurate, or omit the claim. Do not quietly expand the piece into a researched opinion the author never expressed.

## Audience and boundaries

Infer the audience from supplied material when reasonably clear. Do not default every author to the same profession, industry, or expertise level. Ask only if ambiguity would materially change the drafts.

If the source is identified as paid content, use its public-facing lesson, tension, example, or decision rule. Do not reproduce the complete paid template, prompt pack, or implementation sequence unless the user authorizes that disclosure. Make each social draft useful on its own.

Private names, client details, or confidential figures should not be introduced into public-facing drafts without clear intent. Use an interview question if the intended boundary is unclear and consequential.
