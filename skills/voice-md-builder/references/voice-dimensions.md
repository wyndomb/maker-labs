# Voice dimensions

Analyze each dimension for the written register and, when available, the spoken register. For each, note what this person does that a competent generic writer would not. Skip a dimension when the samples show nothing distinctive about it.

## 1. Stance and relationship to the reader

- Who they are to the reader: peer, guide, practitioner reporting from the field, skeptic, friend.
- Use of "I", "you", and "we". How often they address the reader directly.
- How confident claims are: flat statements, hedged, or deliberately provisional. Which hedges they use, and where.
- How they disagree: head-on, through a story, with evidence, with a question, or with humor.

## 2. Openings

How long-form pieces start: a scene, a confession, a claim, a question, a number, a quote, a problem. How quickly they get to the point. What they never do first.

## 3. Structure and movement

- How an argument is built: problem then solution, story then lesson, claim then evidence, numbered steps, or a winding path that pays off at the end.
- Section and heading habits: headings or not, how they are phrased, how long sections run.
- Transitions: the phrases or moves that carry the reader from one part to the next.
- Use of lists versus prose.
- Handoffs: calls to action, paywall breaks, product or sponsor mentions, and how they lead into them.

## 4. Endings

How pieces close: a takeaway, a question, a callback to the opening, an action, a personal note, a sign-off. What they avoid, such as summarizing everything again.

## 5. Sentences and paragraphs

- Rhythm: mix of short and long sentences, deliberate fragments, one-line paragraphs.
- Paragraph length and how often it varies.
- Punctuation habits: dashes, parentheses, colons, exclamation marks, ellipses, question marks.
- Use the stats script's output here, and confirm each number against close reading.

## 6. Vocabulary

- Signature words and phrases they reuse.
- Coined terms or frameworks they name.
- Register: plain, technical, literary, slangy, mixed. Jargon they explain versus jargon they assume.
- Words and phrases they avoid. Check against common AI-sounding vocabulary such as "delve", "landscape", "leverage", "game-changer", "in today's fast-paced world", "it's not just X, it's Y". Record which ones they never use.
- Non-native or regional features that are part of their voice. Describe them, and tell the reader of `VOICE.md` not to polish them out of the person's own drafts. Do not tell anyone to manufacture errors in new writing.

## 7. Examples, stories, and evidence

- Where examples come from: their own work, clients, history, pop culture, research, analogies from another field.
- How stories are told: length, detail, dialogue, where the lesson lands.
- How they use numbers and sources.
- Analogies and metaphors: which domains they draw from.

## 8. Humor and emotion

Whether humor appears, and what kind: dry, self-deprecating, absurd, wordplay. How openly they show feeling. Where in a piece it shows up.

## 9. Formatting

Bold, italics, emoji, headers, bullet lists, block quotes, links, images, sign-offs, PS lines. Note what appears in long-form specifically.

## 10. Spoken register (when available)

- Natural phrasing and pet expressions.
- How they explain something when nobody is editing: order, analogies, back-tracking.
- How they tell stories out loud.
- Filler words and verbal tics.

## 11. The bridge

Compare the spoken and written registers.

- **Bring into writing**: what makes them sound alive when speaking that their writing should keep, such as a particular analogy habit, directness, or a phrase.
- **Leave out of writing**: filler, repetition, rambling, verbal tics.
- **Already the same**: where the two registers match. This is often the core of the voice.
